The established approach to phonics doesn't work for 1 in 5

Schools use a method called Systematic Synthetic Phonics (SSP), which requires kids to learn the sounds of individual letters.

Government data shows that if your child struggles with that approach, they aren’t alone.

  • 1 in 5 don’t meet the expected phonics standard by age six.
  • And around 1 in 4 don’t meet the expected reading standard at age eleven.

The ‘more of the same’ myth

The parents of struggling kids are told that ‘more of the same’ SSP will work, but most of those children never catch up.

  • Only a quarter of pupils who fail phonics in Year 2 manage to meet the expected reading standard by the end of primary.

The limitations of SSP

Too many children make the difficult transition to secondary school without four foundations of reading:

  1. Phonemic awareness

    Children need to be able to isolate, blend and manipulate sounds in spoken words. It's essential – without it, reading and spelling will always elude them.

    To develop this skill, some children need more support than schools can give.

  2. Orthographic knowledge

    To become fluent readers, kids need to understand how to bond speech sounds, spelling, and meaning.

    This isn’t taught. Some kids work it out intuitively. Those that can’t are stuck.

  3. The ability to self-teach

    In the English language there are over four hundred letter-to-sound mappings. Kids are taught the first hundred and are expected to learn the rest themselves.

    Some can intuitively self-teach, while others need support to develop that skill.

  4. Motivation

    ‘Decodable’ books can make reading robotic and restrict vocabulary. If they struggle, kids feel locked in a failure loop.

    It kills their interest and confidence.

Thankfully, there’s a more effective alternative to SSP.

Word mapping: an evolved approach built on proven principles

It starts with speech, not letters

With established phonics, kids attempt to learn the sound of letters in isolation. Word mapping starts instead with the sounds we use to make words. I’ve seen how it can help struggling young learners to finally make progress.

The theoretical basis of word mapping

Word mapping is based on updated reading science about structured linguistic phonics (SLP). It's an innovation that's built on proven principles, including orthographic mapping, phonoemic awareness, and morphological structures.

And it aligns with current UK Government policies – The reading framework (2023) and The writing framework (2025).

How I teach word mapping

I use a brilliant word-mapping framework called Word Mapping Mastery, which was pioneered by Emma Hartnell-Baker.

It introduces kids to speech sounds using fun characters, which they love. It helps them build vital skills that they don't get from SSP, including phonemic awareness and orthographic knowledge, plus the ability to self-teach.

Emma pioneered her approach to teaching word mapping in Australia, where it was adopted by primary schools in South Australia and Queensland.

She returned to UK in 2020 to campaign for a better approach to literacy. In 2026 she secured Government funding for a pilot that will focus initially on earlier identification of children with Special Educational Needs (SEN), then on providing effective interventions.

For the last for four years, while Emma's been campaigning, I've been using her word mapping framework to help struggling learners. I've seen first-hand how, as kids explore words of interest, they discover the joy of reading and writing. And they build self belief that they take back into the classroom.

How word mapping differs from established phonics

Topic Systematic Synthetic Phonics (SSP) Word Mapping Mastery

Theoretical model

Systematic Synthetic Phonics (SSP): Simplified View of Reading, first published by Gough and Tunmer in 1986.

Word Mapping Mastery: Newer models, including the Active View of Reading, first published by Duke and Cartwright in 2021.

Starting point

Systematic Synthetic Phonics (SSP): Letters (print-to-speech).

Word Mapping Mastery: Speech sounds (speech-to-print), which help kids to think about how reading works.

Isolating, blending, and manipulating sounds in spoken words

Systematic Synthetic Phonics (SSP): Phonemic awareness is taught only in reception.

Word Mapping Mastery: It’s embeded into the approach until it sticks.

Bonding speech, spelling, and meaning

Systematic Synthetic Phonics (SSP): Orthographic mapping is not explicitly taught.

Word Mapping Mastery: It’s taught from the start, building reading and spelling fluency.

Ability to self-teach

Systematic Synthetic Phonics (SSP): Kids are given coping strategies and workarounds to remember.

Word Mapping Mastery: Kids are equipped to work out unfamiliar spellings and words for themselves.

Use word mapping in school, and at home

As they did in Australia, UK educators are now seeing how word mapping can help struggling pupils.

During term time, I use this approach to tutor children within primary schools, working one-to-one.

And with training and a little practise, parents can use this approach to support their children outside of school, or if educating at home.
